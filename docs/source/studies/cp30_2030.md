# Clean Power 2030: network constraints and balancing costs

By 2030 Great Britain plans to run on clean power, with about 50 GW of offshore wind, much of it off Scotland and East
Anglia, far from where most electricity is used. When the transmission network cannot carry that power south, the
system operator (NESO) pays wind farms to turn down and gas plants and imports elsewhere to turn up. These payments
are **constraint costs**, and every electricity bill pays for them.

NESO's Clean Power 2030 advice (CP30, November 2024) put them at about GBP 3.57 bn<!--
data/CP30/validation_references/neso_balancing_benchmarks.csv: neso_constraint_cost_gbpm --> in 2030 if the network it
recommends is built on time, and much more if it is late. This study estimates the same costs independently, with the
open-source PyPSA-GB model on the full GB transmission network, for three versions of the 2030 network.

**Why the comparison means something.** PyPSA-GB is independent of NESO's own models. It is built from CP30's published
capacities and energy, which it reproduces before any network limit is applied (generation within 3% of CP30,
{ref}`below <cp30-stage1-check>`), and the same method run on 2020 gives a constraint cost 1.29 times NESO's
outturn ({doc}`validation_2020`). The comparison is with NESO's thermal constraint costs: the cost of keeping power
flows within the network's thermal limits, which is what this model represents.

:::{dropdown} Terms used on this page

NESO
: The National Energy System Operator, which runs the GB electricity system in real time.

CP30, FFR
: NESO's Clean Power 2030 advice to government (November 2024) and its data workbook. FFR, Further Flex and
  Renewables, is the CP30 pathway modelled here.

ES1, ES5, CP.08, CP.18, CP.32
: Sheets of the CP30 data workbook: annual energy by technology (ES1), capacity by region (ES5), clean power turned
  down by zone (CP.08), balancing volumes (CP.18) and the wholesale price duration curve (CP.32).

ETYS, Appendix B
: NESO's Electricity Ten Year Statement: the transmission network model (about 2,000 nodes), and in its Appendix B
  the dated list of planned reinforcements.

Boundary, secured capability
: A boundary is a cut across the network, such as Scotland to England, whose total flow NESO limits. The secured
  capability is that limit: what can flow while the network stays secure if a circuit is lost.

Wholesale market (Stage 1)
: The day-ahead market, which treats GB as one point with no network limits (a "copperplate"); it sets the national
  wholesale price.

Balancing mechanism
: NESO's market for changing generators' output after the wholesale market, here to respect network limits. An
  **offer** is a price to turn up; a **bid** is a price to turn down. **Redispatch** is the energy moved this way.

Constraint cost
: What NESO pays in the balancing mechanism, and in trades with neighbouring countries, to keep power flows within
  network limits.

Interconnector re-trading
: Reversing or reducing a cross-border trade that the wholesale market scheduled but the GB network cannot deliver,
  paid at the value of the trade abroad plus a premium.

CfD, ROC, merchant
: Support for low-carbon generators: Contracts for Difference (a guaranteed strike price), the Renewables Obligation
  (certificates per MWh generated) and no support. Each sets what a wind farm asks to be paid to turn down.

Turn-down, curtailment
: Renewable output not used: turned down in the balancing mechanism because of the network (turn-down), or not
  dispatched at all because supply exceeds demand and exports (curtailment).

Unserved energy, VoLL
: Demand not met; the model prices it at the value of lost load (VoLL, GBP 6,000/MWh) so that it is a last resort.

LOWER, CENTRAL, UPPER
: The three network cases below, named for the cost they lead to.
:::

## The question

- What do constraint and balancing actions cost in 2030 under CP30's FFR pathway?
- How does that cost change with how much of the planned network is built by 2030?
- Where does the model agree with NESO, and where and why does it differ?

### Two stages, as the GB market works

Each month of 2030 is solved in two stages, mirroring how the GB market runs
({doc}`/model/dispatch/wholesale`, {doc}`/model/dispatch/balancing`):

1. **The wholesale market (Stage 1).** Generators, storage and interconnectors are scheduled at least cost as if the
   network had no limits, the "copperplate" on which GB's single national price is set. This stage is shared by all
   three network cases.
2. **The balancing mechanism (Stage 2).** The same schedule is checked against the real network: about 2,000 nodes,
   with each boundary held to its secured capability ({doc}`/model/network/constraints`). Where power cannot flow,
   units are moved off their Stage 1 schedule at the least balancing cost: plant behind a congested boundary is
   turned down (bids), plant on the other side turned up (offers), and cross-border trades re-traded.

What each unit asks to be paid follows its costs and support ({doc}`/model/dispatch/bids_offers_support`). A gas
plant offers to turn up at 1.6 times its fuel and carbon cost. A wind farm on a CfD asks to be paid the support it
loses by turning down, so its bid follows its contract; a ROC wind farm asks for the certificates it gives up. A
cross-border trade is reversed at its value abroad plus a premium of GBP 20/MWh
({doc}`/model/dispatch/interconnector_trading`).

### Three network cases

Only the network in the balancing stage changes between the cases; the fleet and the wholesale market are the same.

| Case | Family | What is built by 2030 | NESO's figure |
|---|---|---|---|
| LOWER | `cp30_network` | CP30's recommended network, including the three projects CP30 says must be accelerated: Norwich-Tilbury (two projects) and Sea Link | GBP 3.57 bn<!-- data/CP30/validation_references/neso_balancing_benchmarks.csv: neso_constraint_cost_gbpm --> |
| CENTRAL | `no_acceleration` | As LOWER, but Norwich-Tilbury and Sea Link on their published dates (2031-33), so not there in 2030 | GBP 7.79 bn<!-- data/CP30/validation_references/neso_balancing_benchmarks.csv: neso_cost_no_acceleration_gbpm --> |
| UPPER | `no_upgrades` | No transmission reinforcement after 2025: every later planned upgrade removed, and every boundary at its 2025 limit | GBP 12.7 bn<!-- data/CP30/validation_references/neso_balancing_benchmarks.csv: neso_cost_no_upgrades_gbpm --> |

Norwich-Tilbury is a new overhead line from Norfolk to the Thames estuary, and Sea Link an undersea cable from Suffolk
to Kent. Both carry East Anglia's offshore wind towards London. NESO's LOWER figure is from the CP30 main report
(Table 1); the CENTRAL and UPPER figures are the upper ends of the ranges the National Audit Office quotes from NESO's
CP30 work (GBP 6.58-7.79 bn and 10.9-12.7 bn; *Upgrading the electricity transmission network*, 2026, Figure 4 and
paragraph 2.8). All are thermal constraint costs in 2024 prices.

Each case is twelve monthly solves, `CP30_FFR_2030_<Mon>_<family>`, summed to a year, `CP30_2030_<family>`.

### How the cost is measured

The model's **constraint cost** is measured as NESO's is: accepted offers and bids of GB plant plus the cost of
re-trading interconnectors. Two things the model also reports are kept out of it, because NESO's figure does not
contain them: unserved energy, a last resort priced at VoLL that is about 10 MWh a year in every case, and the
turn-down of small units that are not in the balancing mechanism, which the model must turn down at a penalty price.

**How wind turn-down is shared.** When a boundary is congested, the model turns down whichever wind farms behind it
are cheapest to turn down first. NESO's CP30 work instead shares a zone's turn-down across all its wind farms. The
energy turned down is the same either way, but the cost differs, because the farms' bids differ. Every month is
therefore costed both ways: as solved, and with each zone's wind turn-down shared pro rata to each farm's
wholesale-market output, at each farm's own bid (`scripts/market/turn_down_allocation.py`). Both are shown; the
**headline** for each case is the one closer to NESO's own figure for that network.

What the table shows:

- **The model reproduces NESO's scale and ordering.** On the recommended network (LOWER) NESO's GBP 3.57 bn lies between
  the model's two ways of sharing turn-down (GBP 3.38-3.88 bn). With Norwich-Tilbury and Sea Link late (CENTRAL) the cost
  roughly doubles, and with no upgrades after 2025 (UPPER) it triples. In both delay cases the model's pro rata figure
  lies inside the range NESO published (GBP 6.58-7.79 bn and 10.9-12.7 bn).
- **Two late projects cost GBP 3.3-3.7 bn in 2030 alone.** That is the difference between LOWER and CENTRAL on either
  basis (GBP 3,259 m as solved, 3,738 m pro rata; NESO's figures differ by GBP 4,220 m)<!-- 6,634 - 3,375 and
  7,622 - 3,884 from the rows above; 7,790 - 3,570 -->. Every other part of the network is the same in the two cases.
- **About half the cost is re-trading interconnectors.** In the wholesale market GB exports when its wind is strong;
  when that wind sits behind a congested boundary, the trade cannot be delivered and NESO must buy it back at its value
  abroad. Re-trading is 58% of the LOWER cost as solved (GBP 1,973 m of 3,375 m), 56% of CENTRAL's and 51% of
  UPPER's<!-- interconnector re-trading / constraint cost as solved, rows above -->.
- **How turn-down is shared matters.** Sharing each zone's turn-down across all its wind farms, as NESO does, adds
  GBP 0.5-1.2 bn a year<!-- pro rata minus as solved in each column: 509, 988, 1,151 -->, because it moves turn-down
  from farms with low bids onto farms whose support makes turning down dearer.
- **Supply stays secure.** Even in UPPER, nearly two-fifths of demand is moved between plant by the balancing
  mechanism, but unserved energy is about 10 MWh a year, against 325 TWh of
  demand<!-- resources/analysis/CP30_2030_no_acceleration_annual_summary.json: aggregate.demand_MWh -->. The cost of
  a late network is money, not blackouts.

## What to bear in mind

- **Perfect foresight within each window.** The wholesale market is solved a day ahead and the balancing mechanism
  in rolling windows, each knowing demand, wind and solar exactly. There is no forecast error, so the cost of
  balancing surprises in real time is not included.
- **Thermal limits only.** The model respects circuits' thermal ratings and NESO's secured boundary capabilities, not
  voltage or stability limits. NESO's figures above are thermal constraint costs too, so the comparison is like for
  like, but NESO's total balancing spend is higher.
- **Bids and offers are derived, not observed.** Offers follow fuel and carbon costs with a mark-up; CfD wind bids the
  support it would lose. The result is sensitive to these choices, and to how turn-down is shared within a zone (GBP
  0.5-1.2 bn a year).
- **Interconnector re-trading rests on inference.** It is about half the cost in every case, priced at the trade's
  value abroad plus a premium. NESO does not publish its own counter-trading cost separately.
- **One weather year.** All results use 2013 weather, CP30's own. A windier year would raise constraint costs, a
  calmer one lower them.
- **The network cases are stylised.** CENTRAL removes three named projects; UPPER removes every upgrade after 2025 but
  keeps any circuit a 2030 generator needs to connect. Neither is a forecast of what will be built.
- **NESO's CENTRAL and UPPER figures are the upper ends of published ranges** (GBP 6.58-7.79 bn and 10.9-12.7 bn).

## How to reproduce

In `config/config.yaml`, load the study and name the network cases to run:

```yaml
studies:
  - cp30_2030
run_scenarios:
  - cp30_network
  - no_acceleration
  - no_upgrades
```

Each name runs its twelve months; the shared build `CP30_FFR_2030` and the shared wholesale-market months (`stage1`)
are built as their inputs. Then:

```bash
snakemake -n            # check what will run
snakemake -j 4          # build, wholesale market, balancing, annual totals and checks
```

Before the first run you need the 2013 weather data (`snakemake -s Snakefile_cutouts`) and a Gurobi licence; the CP30
data workbook is downloaded from NESO by rule `retrieve_neso_document`. See {doc}`/getting_started/running_the_workflow`.
Expect days of computing, not hours: each case is twelve month-long solves of the full network.
