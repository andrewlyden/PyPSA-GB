Core distinction

Central dispatch changes the schedule

A system operator chooses plant output while respecting network limits. It does not, by itself, specify the price paid to generators.

Core distinction

Pay as bid changes settlement

Accepted units receive their own accepted offer. It does not, by itself, determine whether plant self-schedule or are centrally scheduled.

Core distinction

Ownership changes who receives money

Public ownership can internalise market rents and balancing transfers. It does not automatically create either central dispatch or cost-based pricing.

The clean way to read every resultAsk four separate questions: Who owns the asset? Who determines its schedule? What is paid for accepted energy? How are capital and fixed costs recovered? Combining them too early causes double counting.
Conceptual map

Four independent design axes

Real market designs choose one position on each axis. The model experiments discussed here are particular combinations—not a single line from “market” to “state”.

1

Ownership

Private, mixed public–private, public new build, or fully public.

Determines who supplies capital and retains surplus.

2

Scheduling

Self-dispatch or central dispatch; perfect foresight or forecast-based.

Determines who commits and positions plant.

3

Energy settlement

Pay as clear, pay as bid, administered cost, or bilateral contract.

Determines the energy payment rule.

4

Revenue adequacy

Merchant rents, CfDs, capacity payments, regulated return or public tariff.

Determines how fixed and capital costs are recovered.

What each formulation actually solves

Day ahead

National copperplate market

Plant is scheduled by marginal cost and bids without internal transmission constraints. Demand buses share one wholesale price.

→
Gate closure

Positions are anchored

The wholesale position becomes the commercial baseline. Interconnectors can be fixed to that position.

→
Balancing

Network-constrained redispatch

The model increases and decreases output to make the position physically feasible, paying bid/offer prices.

consumer cost = national-price energy + balancing / constraint cost

Forecast

Day-ahead uncertainty

Wind, solar and demand are perturbed with correlated errors calibrated to national forecast RMSE.

→
Central schedule

Real network in Stage 1

A 24-hour LP chooses a least-cost network-feasible schedule. It has no unit commitment, reserve or ramping.

→
Outturn

Real-time correction

The outturn network is reloaded and re-optimised. Zero mark-ups make this a resource-cost correction, not a strategic BM.

payment = national-price energy + cost recovery + firm access + real-time resource correction

Why make-whole payments existA national energy price can be below the cost of a generator dispatched for a local network need. Cost recovery covers incremental scheduled output when marginal cost exceeds the national price. Firm access compensates profitable output in the self-dispatch counterfactual that the central schedule removes.

Same physical central schedule

The forecast-based, network-constrained two-stage dispatch is retained, including the real-time correction.

Different merit-order basis

The operator minimises resource cost. Subsidy and certificate transfers are stripped out, renewable cost is floored at £0.01/MWh, and fuel, carbon and variable O&M drive the ranking.

public tariff requirement = physical resource cost + allowed fixed/capital recovery − public receipts

This is coherent only because the relevant domestic transfers are inside one public account. It should not be presented as the settlement offered to private investors.

No new dispatch solve

The accepted quantities, wholesale prices and all behaviour are frozen at the private pay-as-clear result.

A revenue subtraction

For private non-CfD generators only, positive wholesale margin above short-run cost is removed. CfD assets are excluded because their top-up would rise when wholesale revenue falls.

ceiling = Σ max(wholesale revenue − fuel & carbon − variable O&M, 0)

This is not a forecast of a pay-as-bid market.It is the largest saving available under the deliberately unrealistic assumption that bids, dispatch, entry, exit and replacement support do not respond.

Why “everyone bids cost” is not a stable private-market resultA generator paid only its own short-run cost cannot recover capital or fixed operating cost from the energy market. Rational bidders therefore shade toward the price they expect to clear, or policymakers must supply revenue through CfDs, capacity payments, regulated returns or audited cost recovery. The 2022 REMA consultation consequently linked pay as bid to much deeper changes such as central dispatch and a mandatory pool; the government later declined to take it forward.

Which formulation fits which ownership structure?

“Best suited” here means internally coherent with the ownership and revenue model—not necessarily politically preferred. Any column can be designed differently if the missing incentives and transfers are explicitly replaced.

The key ownership insightCentral dispatch has a smaller incremental financial effect after full nationalisation because the public account already internalises domestic constraint payments and inframarginal rent. Its remaining case is operational efficiency and system security—not capturing the same transfers twice.
Reporting discipline

What is safe to quote

Central dispatch

“On the fixed CP30 2030 case, FTI-style settlement is £1.448bn below the paired self-dispatch settlement. This is mainly avoided constraint payments, not measured domestic resource efficiency.”

Public central dispatch

“Within the fully nationalised tariff, switching dispatch institution lowers the modelled bill by £16.21, but roughly £14.86 is an interconnector-price artefact; the domestic component is around £1.35.”

Pay-as-bid ceiling

“The £3.642bn system / £38.67 household result is a frozen-behaviour upper bound on removable non-CfD margin, not an estimate of pay-as-bid reform.”

Never add all three numbers.The central-settlement saving, nationalised-dispatch change and pay-as-bid ceiling answer different counterfactual questions and overlap in the transfers they remove.
